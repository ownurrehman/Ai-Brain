# Cervical Spondylosis Exercises: Neck Relief Techniques That Work

Cervical spondylosis is the medical term for age-related wear and tear that affects the discs and joints in your neck. Over time, this degenerative condition can cause neck stiffness, pain, headaches, and even numbness or tingling that radiates into your arms. While cervical spondylosis is a natural part of aging, the symptoms are manageable with the right exercise program. At Tonic Physio in Milton, our physiotherapists design targeted exercise protocols that reduce pain, restore neck mobility, and prevent the condition from worsening.

## What Is Cervical Spondylosis

Cervical spondylosis occurs when the discs between the vertebrae in your neck lose hydration and elasticity. The edges of the vertebrae may develop bony growths called osteophytes, and the joints between vertebrae can become inflamed. These changes narrow the space where nerve roots exit the spine, sometimes causing compression that leads to arm pain, numbness, or weakness.

Not everyone with cervical spondylosis experiences symptoms. Many people over fifty show signs of degeneration on imaging but feel no pain. However, once symptoms develop, they tend to recur unless properly managed with targeted therapy.

## Why Exercise Is the Best Treatment for Cervical Spondylosis

Medication and injections can temporarily reduce pain, but they do not address the underlying mechanical problems that cause cervical spondylosis symptoms. Strengthening the deep neck flexor muscles improves head posture and reduces strain on the cervical discs. Stretching tight chest and shoulder muscles relieves tension that pulls the neck into a forward position. Mobilizing stiff joints through controlled movement restores normal mechanics and decreases pain signals.

## Exercise 1: Deep Neck Flexor Strengthening

The deep neck flexors are small muscles at the front of your neck that stabilize the cervical spine. When these muscles are weak, larger neck muscles compensate, creating chronic tension and compression.

Lie on your back with your head resting comfortably. Gently tuck your chin downward as if making a double chin. Hold for five seconds, then release. Repeat ten to fifteen times. This movement activates the deep stabilizers without stressing the larger muscles.

If this exercise causes dizziness or sharp pain, stop and consult your physiotherapist at Tonic Physio. Modified versions can be used for patients with severe disc compression or instability.

## Exercise 2: Chin Tucks Against Resistance

Once you have mastered basic chin tucks, progress to resisted chin tucks to build strength.

Place a small folded towel behind your head against a wall. Gently press your head back into the towel while maintaining a chin-tuck position. Hold for five seconds and repeat ten times. The wall provides resistance that strengthens both the front and back neck muscles in a balanced way.

This exercise also reinforces proper head posture, which reduces forward head posture, a common contributor to cervical spondylosis pain.

## Exercise 3: Upper Trapezius Stretch

Tight upper trapezius muscles pull the neck into a forward and tilted position, increasing pressure on the cervical discs.

Sit upright. Tilt your left ear toward your left shoulder until you feel a gentle stretch along the right side of your neck. Hold for thirty seconds and repeat three times on each side. Do not bounce or force the stretch. Move slowly into the position and breathe steadily.

Your physiotherapist may recommend additional stretches for the levator scapulae and sternocleidomastoid muscles if these are also contributing to your symptoms.

## Exercise 4: Scapular Squeezes

Poor shoulder posture puts extra strain on the neck. Scapular squeezes strengthen the rhomboids and middle trapezius, which pull the shoulders back into a neutral position.

Sit or stand with your arms at your sides. Squeeze your shoulder blades together and downward, as if trying to hold a pencil between them. Hold for five seconds and repeat fifteen times. This movement reduces the forward pull on the neck and relieves compression on the cervical spine.

## Exercise 5: Doorway Pectoral Stretch

Tight chest muscles shorten in response to prolonged sitting and forward head posture. The doorway pectoral stretch lengthens these muscles and allows the shoulders and neck to sit in a neutral position.

Place your forearms on either side of a doorway with your elbows at shoulder height. Step forward gently until you feel a stretch across the front of your chest. Hold for thirty seconds and repeat three times.

## Exercise 6: Prone Y Extensions

Prone Y extensions strengthen the lower trapezius and improve the alignment of the shoulder girdle, which reduces compensatory strain on the neck.

Lie face down on a mat with your forehead resting on a towel. Extend your arms overhead in a Y shape with your thumbs pointing up. Gently lift your arms a few inches off the floor while keeping your shoulder blades engaged. Hold for three seconds and lower slowly. Repeat twelve to fifteen times.

## Exercise 7: Thoracic Spine Mobilization

Stiffness in the upper back forces the neck to compensate by overworking. Thoracic spine mobilization restores movement in the upper back and decreases the demand on the cervical spine.

Sit on a chair with the back of the chair at mid-back level. Place your hands behind your head and gently lean back over the chair edge until you feel a stretch in your upper back. Hold for three seconds and repeat ten times.

Your physiotherapist may use manual therapy techniques to mobilize the thoracic spine before you begin home exercises.

## Exercise 8: Isometric Neck Holds

Isometric exercises build neck strength without moving the neck, which is valuable during acute flare-ups or when movement increases pain.

Place your hand against your forehead and gently press your head into your hand without allowing your head to move. Hold for five seconds and repeat ten times. Repeat with your hand on the back of your head, on the right side, and on the left side.

This exercise strengthens all four sides of the neck evenly without stressing the joints.

## Exercise 9: Supine Rotation Mobilization

Supine rotation mobilization restores normal rotation in the neck, which is often restricted in cervical spondylosis.

Lie on your back with a small rolled towel under your neck. Slowly turn your head to the right as far as comfortable. Hold for five seconds and return to center. Repeat to the left. Perform ten repetitions on each side.

If rotation causes arm pain or numbness, stop immediately and inform your therapist. Modified ranges may be necessary.

## How Often Should You Do These Exercises

For the best results, perform these exercises daily during the first four to six weeks. Once your symptoms improve, reduce to three or four times per week for maintenance. Consistency is more important than intensity. Doing these exercises regularly every day produces better outcomes than doing them intensely once per week.

Your physiotherapist at Tonic Physio will adjust the frequency, intensity, and progression based on your response to treatment.

## When to See a Physiotherapist

If you experience severe arm weakness, loss of hand coordination, difficulty walking, or progressive numbness, seek medical attention immediately. These symptoms may indicate significant nerve compression that requires specialized treatment.

If your neck pain persists beyond two weeks, recurs frequently, or interferes with your ability to work or sleep, book a physiotherapy assessment at Tonic Physio. ## Preventing Cervical Spondylosis Progression

While you cannot reverse aging, you can slow the progression of cervical spondylosis by maintaining good posture, taking frequent breaks from sitting, sleeping with a supportive pillow, strengthening your neck and upper back muscles, and managing stress to reduce muscle tension.

Regular physiotherapy checkups help identify early changes before they become painful problems.
